The country beneath the crossing

Before Britain was an island, people lived where the North Sea rolls.

The sea between Britain and continental Europe looks like a permanent boundary. It is a comparatively recent arrangement. Beneath parts of the North Sea lie the remains of a landscape that once connected the shores: rivers, changing coastlines and environments used by prehistoric people. Archaeologists call that drowned region Doggerland.

It has no surviving capital that can summarise the whole story. Its evidence is scattered through seabed sediments, recovered objects and reconstructed terrain. A familiar version ends with a single enormous wave. The research reveals a more unsettling history: a lived-in landscape repeatedly transformed, with its final disappearance occurring unevenly rather than at one simple, universal moment.

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The sea gives pieces back

The Rijksmuseum van Oudheden’s research programme describes tools, worked bone and antler, and human and animal remains recovered from the North Sea. Fishing nets and sand brought ashore for beach projects have returned fragments of the lost landscape. Some objects carry clear signs of human workmanship even when their original location has been disturbed.

That disturbance creates the central difficulty. An object lifted from a seabed or found in redeposited beach sand may have lost the neighbours and sediment layers that would explain its setting. It can establish past activity without identifying an intact settlement. Archaeologists must work from those fragments toward locations where objects and environmental evidence can still be studied together.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A forest becomes readable

A 2026 study led by Robin Allaby examined ancient DNA in 252 sediment samples from 41 cores along a prehistoric river system in southern Doggerland. The team combined DNA with sediment analysis to distinguish more secure local signals from mixed material. Fine deposits and coarse, reworked deposits cannot be interpreted in exactly the same way.

The secure signals indicated temperate trees including oak, elm and hazel more than 16,000 years ago. Such woodland could have offered resources in a region often pictured as harsh Ice Age terrain. The researchers propose favourable refuges for plants and animals, potentially for people as well. This reconstructs an environment; it does not reveal a human village merely because tree DNA survives.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The catastrophe with an aftermath

The Storegga tsunami, around 6200 BCE, was a real event generated by a vast submarine landslide off Norway. James Walker and colleagues published evidence from the southern North Sea in 2020. Their analysis challenged the assumption that its arrival necessarily caused the definitive, wholesale drowning of Doggerland. Local topography changes how a wave reaches, damages and retreats from a coast.

Gradual sea-level rise belongs in the same story. Bradford’s later landscape simulation makes that changing geography visible across thousands of years, while the 2026 environmental research supports survival of some land after the tsunami. A catastrophic wave and a landscape’s eventual inundation are related questions, but they are not automatically the same event.

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

The lost landscape is real; its ending was complex

Archaeological finds and environmental records establish a drowned prehistoric world. The Storegga tsunami is documented, but the cited studies do not support reducing every part of Doggerland’s disappearance to one wave. The identity and lives of particular communities remain much harder to reconstruct than the former landscape.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

The next discovery may look far less dramatic than a monument rising from the water. A securely located tool beside dated sediments, plant remains and an occupation surface could tell researchers more about daily life than an impressive object stripped of context. Linking evidence together is what turns an underwater landscape into a history of its inhabitants.

Doggerland also changes the map of what is missing. Present shorelines cannot define the whole prehistoric world because many former places lie offshore. Recovering them expands the area in which ancient lives can be investigated. It does not establish a hidden industrial civilisation; it gives real prehistoric communities back a portion of the land they once inhabited.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST

Locate intact occupation deposits, date their sediment sequences and connect recovered objects with local environmental records before inferring particular settlements or population movements.
