Stargate: the spies who tried to see without looking

The psychic spying programme was real. Its claimed power is another question.

A secret installation is valuable precisely because outsiders cannot see inside it. Imagine an intelligence collector who could describe that installation without entering it, photographing it or intercepting a message. That was the extraordinary possibility behind remote viewing: attempting to obtain information about a distant target through mental impressions rather than ordinary senses.

This was more than a rumour attached to the CIA. The agency says it began research at Stanford Research Institute in 1972. Other American defence and intelligence organisations later carried the work forward. The unsettling part of the story is already documented: public institutions spent years investigating a capability that sounded supernatural. Whether they found it requires a different kind of evidence.

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

An extraordinary claim enters the laboratory

In 1974, researchers Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff published a paper in Nature describing experiments conducted under sensory shielding. They argued that their findings suggested a perceptual ability beyond the usual channels. Publication made the claim available for scientific scrutiny; it did not turn the proposed explanation into an established human capacity.

The distinction matters because remote viewing contained two questions. Could a participant produce descriptions that judges rated unusually well against a target? And, if that happened, had ordinary information, experimental weaknesses and other explanations been excluded? An interesting score is the beginning of that investigation. Calling it psychic perception already supplies the answer.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The file that records an impression

One surviving 1980 session record concerns the American hostages in Iran. It preserves the attempt to obtain information through a viewer's impressions. The document explicitly treats the session as raw intelligence, leaving interpretation and evaluation to the organisation requesting it. An archive can therefore prove that a session happened without proving that its contents described the target accurately.

That qualification is easy to lose when a declassified page is presented as a revelation. The institutional stamp authenticates a record of what people did, requested or reported. It does not independently authenticate every statement inside. To demonstrate useful intelligence, the impressions would need to be checked against information unavailable to the viewer, with successes and failures counted under consistent rules.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

Two tests for a remarkable possibility

In 1995, the programme returned to CIA scrutiny. The agency commissioned the American Institutes for Research to examine laboratory work and operational applications. The review brought in a statistician and a psychologist with contrasting positions on remote viewing. A programme that had moved through several institutions now faced an assessment designed to distinguish intriguing experiments from an intelligence capability.

There were two tests to apply. First, did the laboratory evidence establish an effect, and could its cause be distinguished from ordinary explanations? Second, did the operational material provide information specific and dependable enough to be useful? Those questions could produce different answers. A striking statistical result would not automatically locate a hostage, and an apparently helpful description would not automatically explain how it had been obtained.

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

A real programme is not proof of a paranormal mechanism

The records support the existence of remote-viewing research and operational experiments. The commissioned review found laboratory results worth examining but no unambiguous demonstration of their paranormal origin, and no sufficient operational usefulness to justify continuation. Operational descriptions were too vague, inconsistent or dependent on interpretation to provide the specific, dependable intelligence required. The CIA declined to restart the programme.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

The laboratory disagreement should remain visible. It is inaccurate to pretend that every reviewer interpreted every result identically. It is equally inaccurate to treat a disagreement over statistics as an established paranormal discovery.

A convincing new claim would need repeatable performance under controls that prevent ordinary information transfer, followed by independently verified usefulness. The historical programme cannot substitute for that demonstration.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST

Can an independently run, preregistered test reproduce useful target information while excluding ordinary information channels?
