The Philadelphia Experiment: the ship that vanished in a letter

An invisibility claim surfaced in letters. What could the Navy's logs show?

The story promises a secret experiment with consequences its inventors could not control: a warship made invisible, transported between ports, and a crew catastrophically harmed. Its alleged setting is the Second World War. Its supporting trail, however, emerges years later through letters and an unusually annotated book.

That gap is the first useful clue. The Philadelphia Experiment must be tested as a historical claim about a particular ship, in particular places, during a particular period. Before asking whether the Navy possessed a fantastic machine, it is worth asking whether USS Eldridge was even where the story puts it.

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A correspondent describes a vanished ship

After UFO author Morris K. Jessup published his 1955 book, a correspondent using the name Carlos Miguel Allende sent him an extraordinary account. Allende alleged that a wartime naval experiment had made a ship invisible and transported it between Philadelphia and Norfolk. The voyage, he claimed, had taken place with terrible consequences for members of the crew.

The alleged participant was USS Eldridge. The story also named a merchant vessel, SS Andrew Furuseth, as a vantage point for witnesses. These details gave the account something more concrete than an unnamed secret: identifiable ships whose histories could be investigated. Yet the material reaching Jessup was correspondence about an alleged event years earlier, rather than a wartime experimental report.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The book enters a naval office

An anonymously annotated copy of Jessup's book subsequently arrived at the Office of Naval Research. Two officers became interested in the comments and their apparent connection to the correspondence. The ONR account says they arranged a small private reproduction of the annotated material. The story now had a physical object associated with a naval office, as well as its named ships.

That association helps explain its appeal. An unusual claim circulating among people connected to naval research can feel as though it has crossed a threshold from rumour to classified knowledge. But several distinct events need to be tracked: receiving a book, becoming curious about its annotations, and conducting the wartime experiment those annotations describe. The questions raised by the officers' interest could be checked against the institutional chronology.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A real naval technology and a testable itinerary

There was also a genuine naval technology involving electrical systems and a ship's magnetic signature: degaussing. It helped protect vessels against magnetic mines, and naval engineers continue to work on magnetic-signature reduction. This provides a separate, documented research thread to examine alongside Allende's claims. Its actual purpose and effects can be compared with the extraordinary capabilities described in the letters.

Eldridge's published service history records a destroyer escort commissioned in August 1943 and later assigned to Mediterranean convoy protection. More detailed records offered a way to test the alleged journey. The relevant questions were concrete: where was Eldridge during the claimed period, where was Andrew Furuseth, and were the two ships together when the supposed witnesses said they were? The logs could answer without assuming a mechanism for the alleged disappearance.

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

The central claim fails its checkable historical test

The allegation has a traceable history in postwar correspondence. Its named ship's documented movements contradict the claimed Philadelphia setting. Officer interest in the letters and the existence of degaussing do not authenticate the extraordinary event. The Navy's reviewed deck logs and war diary place Eldridge away from Philadelphia in the relevant 1943 period; Andrew Furuseth was not alongside it at Norfolk as alleged. ONR itself was established in 1946. Degaussing reduces magnetic signatures, not optical visibility, and does not teleport a vessel.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

The precise way every element of the legend developed is less certain than the ship's whereabouts. A possible conflation should not be promoted into a proven explanation for every later embellishment. The Navy offers degaussing as one possible source of confusion, rather than a demonstrated origin for the whole story.

A historical claim could be reconsidered if authentic contemporary evidence emerged. It would need to survive comparison with the existing logs, rather than requiring all conflicting records to be dismissed simply because they conflict.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST

Is there an authenticated wartime record that independently supports the alleged event and reconciles the ships' documented movements?
