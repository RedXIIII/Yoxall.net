Cottingley: the fairies that survived a photograph

Two Yorkshire cousins presented fairy photographs. Why did a famous author believe them?

Beside a beck behind a Yorkshire home, two cousins produced photographs they presented as images of fairies. Elsie Wright and Frances Griffiths made their first two pictures in 1917 and three more in 1920. An ordinary family camera had supplied something their advocates regarded as extraordinary: a visible record of beings usually confined to stories and reported sightings.

One advocate was Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes. He helped turn the photographs into a public question: could a camera register something that most people never saw? His reputation added attention, but it was the images themselves, and the explanations offered for them, that the case depended on. What had persuaded a writer so closely associated with a fictional detective?

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

From a family photograph to a public claim

Bradford's museum account follows the photographs through the Theosophical movement and Edward Gardner to Doyle. Gardner investigated, and photographic negatives became central to the discussion. An expert assessment that they showed no retouching offered the advocates a technical reason to take the claim seriously, beyond accepting the cousins' statements about what they had seen.

The two early pictures presented different scenes. In one, Frances appeared beside the fairy figures; in the other, Elsie posed with a figure described as a gnome. The latter survives in a museum collection as a photograph dated September 1917. These were tangible objects that could be enlarged, inspected and circulated, giving the debate something more persistent than a reported encounter.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

Read the believer's case in his own words

Doyle's 1922 book, The Coming of the Fairies, preserves his side of the argument through photographs and correspondence. He treated assurances about the negatives, together with his assessment of the cousins' abilities and circumstances, as reasons for belief. The book records how an advocate assembled a case. Its account of his confidence should be read as his position, not as a fact about the beings he proposed.

He also pursued technical opinions and reports from Gardner's visits. Doyle was interested in whether the pictures could be dismissed as a photographic trick, and whether the people who produced them possessed the skills such a trick would require. The investigation combined examination of objects with judgements about character. For the reader following it, the crucial question is what those different kinds of inquiry actually tested.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A second series raises the stakes

When the cousins were together again in 1920, Gardner supplied new photographic equipment, and three more pictures followed. In his book, Doyle describes receiving the new prints while in Australia and becoming more confident in the claim. The additional images appeared, to him, to strengthen a case that no longer depended on just one occasion beside the beck.

The photographs and cameras later entered museum collections. Conservation work has examined the surviving equipment, including CT scanning with the University of Bradford. The objects make it possible to investigate the history of the photography itself. Before accepting the advocates' conclusion, however, the original question remains: what do authentic photographs establish about the subjects placed before a camera?

Staged photographs; no authenticated fairy evidence

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

The photographic process was not the deception's weak point

The historical photographs and Doyle's advocacy are real. Museum accounts describe staged cutout figures and later admissions. The disputed personal claim about the fifth image supplies no independent basis for treating it as a photograph of a fairy. The admissions emerged in the 1980s. Paper figures held with hatpins could be photographed in a real scene without retouching the negative: the camera faithfully recorded a staged subject.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

There are still historical questions about how the cousins and their advocates understood the episode. Letters and surviving objects can refine that history without reopening a paranormal conclusion unsupported by the evidence. In the later accounts, Frances maintained that the fifth photograph was genuine, while Elsie regarded all five as faked. The disagreement is part of the history, but supplies no independent authentication of the final image.

The case remains useful because its deception worked before digital editing. Authentic pixels, prints or negatives can preserve an inauthentic event; verifying the medium is only part of verifying the claim.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST
