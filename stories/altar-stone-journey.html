The stone that points north

A six-tonne block at Stonehenge carries a distant geological signature.

Among Stonehenge’s stones, one long sandstone block carries a question about distance. The Altar Stone is estimated to weigh six tonnes. Its familiar name may suggest a function, but researchers still question how it originally stood. The more revealing record lies inside the stone: mineral grains with ages and chemistry that can be compared with rocks elsewhere.

For years, its origin was associated with Wales. In 2024, researchers presented a strikingly different match: northeast Scotland’s Orcadian Basin. The change made an already difficult transport question much larger. A geological source can be investigated in a laboratory. The human journey from that region to Salisbury Plain leaves a different, much less complete trail.

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A fingerprint older than the monument

The 2024 team analysed zircon, apatite and rutile grains in Altar Stone fragments. These minerals preserve clues to the older rocks from which the sandstone’s sediment was derived. Comparisons across Britain and Ireland found a strong similarity with Old Red Sandstone from the Orcadian Basin, rather than the previously proposed Anglo-Welsh source.

That result points to a region, not an identified quarry. Sedimentary rocks collect material from earlier landscapes, so investigators compare populations of grains and several kinds of evidence. The published study argued for an origin at least 750 kilometres from Stonehenge. Such a match makes long-distance movement a serious archaeological problem without identifying a single departure point.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The missing travel record

The research team suggested transport by sea as a plausible answer to the practical obstacles of a long overland journey. Curtin’s first-party account emphasised the implications for prehistoric organisation and connections. Those implications are compelling, but a proposed maritime route is not the discovery of the vessel that carried the stone or a chain of dated stopping places.

English Heritage’s stone guide describes the Altar Stone as a large slab of greenish Old Red Sandstone, partly buried beneath fallen stones of the largest trilithon. Whether it originally lay flat or stood upright is uncertain. The central object is therefore surrounded by more than one open question: how it arrived, how it was set and what significance its builders attached to it.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

Could ice have done part of the work?

In 2026, a follow-up compared candidate sandstones with ice-flow models. Caithness gave the closest zircon-age match, but ice movement mostly pointed northeast. A local southeastward pathway toward Dogger Bank could hypothetically shorten the human journey. The analysis tests a scenario; it does not recover a route.

There is a chronological obstacle: Dogger Bank was submerged before the stone’s likely arrival at Stonehenge. The study allows possible intermediate glacial help but requires substantial human movement for final placement. Its natural transport model creates additional constraints rather than solving the journey.

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

The source region is clearer than the journey

Geochemical evidence strongly supports a northeastern Scottish origin. The exact source and transport sequence remain unresolved. Boats, overland movement and possible intermediate glacial dispersal must be assessed against different evidence; the published research does not demonstrate a complete route or remove the requirement for substantial human transport.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

The route would tell a human story as well as an engineering one. Choosing a far-travelled block may reflect relationships and meanings that the rock itself cannot name. Geological analysis can narrow where to look; archaeology must investigate how people selected, worked and moved material through their own landscape.

Source and route should advance together. A better match within the Scottish basin could change which transport scenarios are physically possible. Dated archaeological evidence along a proposed path could strengthen a human itinerary. Until those pieces meet, the remarkable established fact is the distant geological connection, with the journey still awaiting a persuasive reconstruction.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST

Find a closer multi-mineral source match, refine transport and inundation chronologies, and seek archaeological evidence capable of distinguishing proposed human routes.
