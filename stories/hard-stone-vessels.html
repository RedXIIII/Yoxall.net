The jar that makes you look twice

Hard stone, a narrow mouth, and a question hidden inside.

At first it looks like a small container. Then the material changes the picture. The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogues this jar as diorite, made around 3100–2650 BCE. It stands only 5.7 centimetres high. Its opening invites an awkward question: how do you hollow hard stone through a mouth much smaller than the body?

Ben van Kerkwyk’s UnchartedX brings modern metrology to that ancient puzzle: scan the vessels, inspect their curves and ask whether their precision points to lost advanced machining. He argues that it does. Testing that interpretation means looking beyond a photograph. What would the stone have to preserve for the measurements to identify its makers’ tools?

01 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

The surface becomes a dataset

In its 2023 account, UnchartedX described laboratory inspection alongside structured-light and CT scanning of several vessels. This is a productive move away from judging a polished photograph: a scan can expose uneven walls, changing centres and differences between inner and outer surfaces. Those features can become measurements that other investigators can question or repeat.

But geometry does not supply an excavation history. The same post corrected an ownership detail for one large vessel; a 2025 update described scans from the Petrie Museum. Private collections and museum collections require object-specific documentation. An impressive scan is evidence about the scanned shape. Its archaeological age, material history and manufacturing interpretation need additional evidence.

02 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

A comparison changes the question

Looking down a narrow opening, the eye supplies a deceptively simple test: does the interior look smooth? A scan asks harder questions. How round is each cross-section? Do the centres of successive sections line up? Could a series of individually impressive circles still describe a cavity that wanders slightly through the stone?

Now consider the mouth. Can the chosen instrument see every internal surface, or does the opening conceal parts of it? Which regions were measured, and which were reconstructed? A December 2025 study compared 19 Petrie Museum vessels with 49 modern examples. Its assessment turns on these distinctions: geometry, uncertainty and the difference between a measured shape and an intended dimension.

03 / FOLLOWING THE STORY

What abrasive work leaves behind

Experimental archaeologist Denys Stocks investigated early vessel drilling by testing materials and techniques. Experiments matter because they turn an explanation into work that produces a hole, debris and tool wear. They can show that a proposed process is feasible and help identify its traces. Reconstructing one process does not automatically reproduce every vessel or establish one universal method. Matching a polished shape is only part of that comparison; the work itself must leave comparable evidence.

Another clue survives in an Amarna stone fragment examined by Met researchers Anna Serotta and Federico Carò. A drill-hole residue contained corundum-rich abrasive and bronze corrosion products. The abrasive helps explain how a relatively soft metal tool could work demanding stone. This is later New Kingdom evidence, so it cannot simply be transferred to every Predynastic object.

SOURCE REVIEW / 9 October 2026

The craftsmanship survives the argument

The 2025 Petrie sample’s overall geometry overlapped modern handmade comparators. Its interiors combined strong circularity with weaker concentricity, interpreted by the author as consistent with rotational grinding and tool repositioning. These are relative quality scores, not engineering tolerances. The pictured Met jar was not measured. The cited evidence does not demonstrate necessary lost advanced machining or impossibility of modern reproduction.

WHAT REMAINS OPEN

The next question.

The interesting unfinished work is now specific. Which marks remain beneath the polish? Can an experimentally made interior reproduce the same distribution of circularity and displaced centres? A persuasive answer would connect an authenticated object, a documented measurement procedure and a process that leaves matching physical traces.

There is room to investigate exceptional individual vessels without turning a class average into a verdict on every object. There is also room to admire the pictured jar without attaching somebody else’s tolerance claim to it. Its small scale concentrates the achievement: a patterned, stubborn material made into a controlled and delicate form.

A USEFUL NEXT TEST

Measure authenticated vessels independently, publish uncertainty and full inner-surface data, then compare experimentally produced tool traces and geometry against the originals.
