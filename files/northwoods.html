THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

In March 1962, the Joint Chiefs of Staff submitted proposed provocations that could justify U.S. military intervention in Cuba. Suggestions included a ship explosion blamed on Cuba, fabricated attacks around Guantanamo, and destruction of an unmanned substitute aircraft made to resemble a civilian airliner.

The memorandum is authentic, and the proposals are explicit. It was a preliminary planning submission, not an account of attacks that happened. Its later release exposed how senior military planners considered manufacturing a public case for war. Northwoods was not executed and does not prove unrelated later attacks were staged.

Read the memorandum as a planning document. Its language moves from a desired political outcome to fabricated events that could supply the justification. That reversal is the historical shock: the public explanation would have been manufactured to support an action already being contemplated.

An authentic proposal is strong evidence about its authors’ willingness to consider deception. Execution requires a different trail: authorisations, operational records, participants and corroboration. Keeping those questions separate makes the actual document more useful than treating it as a universal explanation for later events.
