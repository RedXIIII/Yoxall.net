THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

Seahenge was a Bronze Age timber monument at Holme next the Sea: an oval circuit of posts surrounding an inverted tree stump. Tree-ring dating places its construction in spring or early summer 2049 BCE, when the location was saltmarsh. Its modern exposure on the shore and excavation in 1999 revealed toolmarks and a structure that normally would not survive for millennia. Its function remains debated; a connection with commemoration or death is an interpretation, not a recovered certainty.

Stone monuments dominate the surviving image of British prehistory because timber usually decays. Seahenge offers a different material world. Preserved oak records both construction choices and the marks of Early Bronze Age tools. The circle enclosed an upside-down stump, making the arrangement visually striking without telling us what its builders thought it meant. Environmental evidence places the structure in a saltmarsh; its present shoreline setting must not be projected unchanged into 2049 BCE. Changing coastal conditions helped reveal a monument whose original landscape had already disappeared.

Norfolk’s archaeological account considers several possible meanings, including marking a death or an event. Dendrochronology contributes firmer evidence by comparing the timbers’ growth rings. Later work on the nearby second timber circle, Holme II, dated its wood to the same spring or summer of 2049 BCE. Two closely situated, contemporary monuments create a more interesting question than an isolated oddity: what relationship did they have? Dating establishes their shared period. It does not establish an exact ceremony, identify a person commemorated, or prove that the central stump was used for a particular funerary practice.

Where the evidence stops.

The date is much firmer than the function. Funerary, commemorative, and ritual explanations remain interpretations; the wood does not establish a specific ceremony or belief system.
