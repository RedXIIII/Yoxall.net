THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

Ancient texts and inscriptions preserved two names while their city lay submerged. Franck Goddio and the European Institute for Underwater Archaeology located the remains in 2000 in Aboukir Bay, roughly 6.5 kilometres off Egypt’s present coast. Temples, harbour basins, statues and inscribed monuments emerged from the surveys and excavations. The finds joined the names: Thonis for the Egyptians, Heracleion for the Greeks. Oxford research catalogued hundreds of recovered statuettes and amulets. This is a documented lost city whose physical remains make the rediscovery tangible, with much archaeology still to explore.

A sunken city is extraordinary without being Atlantis. Underwater survey connects buildings, objects and inscriptions to a particular port at the Nile’s edge. The name and location can be investigated against records as well as against the archaeological landscape.

A submerged site also demands a reconstruction of environmental change. Subsidence, sediment, water and earthquakes can operate over different periods. The evidence for this city does not transfer automatically to another legendary place. It shows why the search for drowned landscapes is serious work when conducted with specific, testable claims.

Where the evidence stops.

Thonis-Heracleion is a historically attested Egyptian port. Its rediscovery does not identify Plato’s Atlantis or establish that every lost-city legend is historical.
