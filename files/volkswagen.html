Dieselgate

The cars behaved cleanly when watched. The software knew the test.

THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

Volkswagen installed software in certain diesel vehicles that detected emissions testing and activated full controls during the test. During ordinary driving, those controls operated less effectively. EPA reported nitrogen oxide emissions as high as forty times the applicable standard for affected 2.0-litre vehicles.

The deception became public in 2015. Volkswagen later pleaded guilty to three federal felony counts covering conspiracy, obstruction, and importation through false statements. The 2017 sentence included a $2.8 billion criminal penalty. Software deliberately made the test result differ from normal operation, and the company concealed the scheme from regulators and customers.

The deception lived inside the difference between a test and normal use. A vehicle could behave one way when software recognised laboratory conditions and another on the road. Customers and regulators were therefore looking at a carefully managed representation of performance.

This makes Dieselgate a useful companion to older covert-operation files: the mechanism is contemporary software, but the paper trail still matters. Independent measurements, enforcement documents and the guilty plea establish what was done. The file concerns the affected systems and vehicles, rather than every diesel engine.
