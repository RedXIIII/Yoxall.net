Stonehenge: the Altar Stone’s long journey

A six-tonne stone points north. Its journey remains a puzzle.

THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

A 2024 Nature study linked the Altar Stone’s mineral-age fingerprint to northeast Scotland’s Orcadian Basin, overturning a Welsh-source assumption. A 2026 study refined possible sources and tested glacial transport, concluding that substantial human movement was still necessary even with possible glacial assistance. The exact source, route, transport method and motivations remain unresolved.

The Altar Stone is a distinctive sandstone block within Stonehenge, rather than one of the famous upright sarsens. Researchers analysed the ages and chemistry of mineral grains in its fragments and compared that fingerprint with possible source rocks. Their 2024 result pointed to the Orcadian Basin in northeast Scotland. Establishing a northern geological source transforms the practical question: how did a stone weighing around six tonnes reach Salisbury Plain, and what connections made such a journey worth undertaking?

A 2026 paper combines provenance analysis with ice-sheet modelling instead of treating the journey as a simple choice between boats and people dragging a rock. Caithness provides the closest zircon-age match among its candidates, but modelled ice flow does not deliver the stone directly to Stonehenge. A possible intermediate glacial stage creates further geographic and timing problems. The authors still require substantial human transport. The compelling mystery concerns the actual sequence of movement and decisions, not a demonstrated impossibility for ancient people.

Where the evidence stops.

A regional provenance is not an identified quarry. Geological fingerprinting does not establish the route, date or transport equipment; the 2026 scenarios are model-based constraints, not observations of the journey.

What would move this forward?

  • Find a source outcrop matching multiple mineral-age and chemical signatures.
  • Test transport scenarios against reconstructed geography, ice flow and sea-level timing.
  • Seek archaeological evidence of quarrying, staging points or stone-moving activity along proposed routes.
