THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

WHO’s SAGO assessment, published on 27 June 2025, concluded that the available evidence favours zoonotic spillover, directly from bats or through an intermediate host. It also said missing information prevents full assessment of all hypotheses, including a laboratory-related origin. That combination is meaningful: an evidential preference with unresolved gaps, rather than equal proof for every account or confirmation of a deliberate release.

Reconstructing a pandemic’s origin depends on samples, sequences, early cases, animal movements and laboratory information that can disappear or remain inaccessible. WHO’s 2025 assessment considered scientific papers, reports, available intelligence statements and expert discussions. Its conclusion favoured zoonotic spillover on the evidence available. That describes the balance of the reviewed evidence; it does not identify every step, the exact animal or a complete chain of transmission beyond reasonable dispute.

WHO also reports that important information was not shared, including more early genetic sequences, detailed market-animal information and information about work and biosafety in Wuhan laboratories. Missing records matter because they constrain how hypotheses can be evaluated. They are not affirmative evidence for whatever scenario a reader already prefers. A laboratory accident, deliberate engineering and intentional release are distinct claims requiring distinct supporting evidence; leaving one possibility unexcluded does not establish the others.

Where the evidence stops.

Not excluded is not confirmed. The cited assessment does not establish a deliberate release, engineering or a specific laboratory accident, and its evidential preference does not resolve every step of the origin.

What would move this forward?

  • Release and independently examine relevant early sequences, case records and market-animal data.
  • Make contemporaneous laboratory inventories, research records and biosafety information available for scrutiny.
  • Compare each proposed origin’s specific predictions with traceable biological and epidemiological evidence.
