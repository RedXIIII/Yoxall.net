THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

The confrontation at Orgreave on 18 June 1984 produced injuries and 95 arrests. Charges were subsequently dropped after police evidence was discredited. On 26 March 2026, the government launched an inquiry with published terms covering police and government planning, the events, their aftermath and the public narrative. Its establishment is documented; its eventual findings must be assessed on their own evidence.

The disturbing question at Orgreave is how a confrontation became an authoritative public account. The government’s launch statement acknowledges that the charges against arrested miners were dropped after police evidence was discredited. That creates a concrete starting point for scrutiny: what police recorded, how prosecutors received it, what decision-makers knew and how events were presented outside the courtroom. The inquiry can examine those connections rather than treating one dramatic photograph as the entire history.

The 2026 terms extend beyond the clash itself to planning by police and government, subsequent events and the impact on individuals and communities. An inquiry can assemble documents and testimony that were dispersed across institutions for decades. Its launch does not establish every allegation campaigners have made, or assign criminal responsibility in advance. The next meaningful change in confidence comes from disclosed records, tested testimony and findings that explain how the evidence supports a particular conclusion.
