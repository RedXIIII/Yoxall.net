THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

Historic England combines aerial photographs and lidar to map archaeological landscapes. Filtered laser-scan data can expose subtle earthworks and features obscured by vegetation; its public explorer connects mapped features with fuller records. New 2026 standards describe systematic interpretation and recording. The terrain is measurable, while a feature’s date and purpose still require archaeological evidence.

A field can look empty because its surviving history is shallow, overgrown or only visible under particular weather conditions. Air photographs reveal cropmarks; lidar measures surface shape and can produce a ground model after vegetation returns are filtered. Historic England illustrates this with an Iron Age enclosure in Savernake Forest. The effect can feel like switching on a second landscape, although the laser records terrain rather than directly seeing through soil to buried rooms.

The Aerial Archaeology Mapping Explorer brings together decades of interpretation across England, with links to monument records and project reports. It is an invitation to investigate particular places, rather than a catalogue of automatically proven mysteries. The 2026 standards emphasise integrated sources, consistent mapping and compatibility with historic environment records. A suspicious rectangle may turn out to be an enclosure, a quarry or a recent disturbance; its shape starts the inquiry, and excavation or other evidence can refine it.

Where the evidence stops.

Lidar does not directly photograph buried interiors, and a geometric pattern alone cannot establish age, function or a lost civilisation. Coverage and interpretation vary by survey area.

What would move this forward?

  • Check a suspected feature against older photographs and independent terrain processing.
  • Compare the map interpretation with field survey, excavation or geophysical evidence.
  • Seek dated material and historical records before assigning a period or purpose.
