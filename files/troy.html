Troy: a city beneath the epic

The city was real. How much of the story was?

THE STORY BEHIND THE HEADLINE

At Hisarlik in northwestern Turkey, excavations revealed successive cities, fortifications and a substantial Bronze Age settlement at the site identified as Troy. This gives the legendary setting a physical reality. Excavation director Manfred Korfmann reported evidence of destruction around 1180 BCE and argued that armed conflicts could lie behind the tradition. The ruins do not establish every episode in Homer. The compelling question is how real places and remembered conflicts became an epic populated by heroes, gods and a famous wooden horse. The archaeology opens that question; it does not close it.

An epic can preserve a geographical memory without becoming a literal excavation report. Hisarlik’s successive settlements make that distinction tangible: a place can be real, its fortifications impressive and its destruction archaeologically visible while the identity of a particular conflict remains disputed.

The layers are not interchangeable. Different building phases and destruction horizons belong to different communities and periods. Archaeologists compare those sequences with texts and regional history; they do not extract a named hero or a wooden horse from a general sign of conflict.

Where the evidence stops.

A real Troy does not verify Achilles, Helen, the wooden horse or every detail of the Iliad. Credit the longer excavation history, rather than a lone discovery myth.
